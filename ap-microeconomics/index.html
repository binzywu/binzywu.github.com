Test a prediction.

These classroom models use fictional quantities and explicit assumptions. They support reasoning, not empirical forecasts. Read the axes, hold one control fixed, and explain why the result changes.

Supply, demand and a tax wedge

Demand is P = 100 − Q and supply is P = 20 + Q. Competitive price-taking market, linear curves, no externality. Tax is per unit; shift changes the demand intercept. Orange is the tax-inclusive supply curve. Compare the buyer price with the seller price.

Predict before moving a control

Hold one control fixed. Predict which curve or quantity changes, identify the mechanism, then compare the result. Explain one limitation of this model.

A competitive firm: output and profit

Price is fixed by the market. Total cost is 40 + 2Q + 0.5Q²; MC = 2 + Q and minimum AVC is 2 at the boundary. This continuous model has no capacity limit. At price below 2, the firm shuts down; at price 2, zero output is selected from the boundary indifference.

Private incentives and external damage

Demand/MSB is P = 100 − Q. Private MC is 20 + Q. Each unit creates constant external damage, so social MC is 20 + Q + damage. A per-unit corrective tax equal to damage reproduces the social quantity in this model. No other distortions are included.

A useful study session

  1. Recall one idea from the previous unit.
  2. Predict the next worked step before revealing it.
  3. Rebuild the example on paper with labeled units or graph axes.
  4. Complete both checks. The lesson list retains ✓ Completed.
  5. Vary one model input and explain the result, then try the unit’s written drill.

Completed checks record practice, not mastery or an AP score.

Read a model carefully

  1. State the question and time horizon.
  2. Label axes and distinguish a stock, flow, rate and percentage.
  3. Identify what is held fixed.
  4. Explain each causal link or mathematical step.
  5. Identify a limitation and evidence that could test the claim.
