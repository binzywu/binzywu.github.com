    MIT Sample Essays Reference (Fall 2027)

    Sample essays and structural guidance for MIT's short-essay application (Fall 2027 entry). See mit-college-research, runchen-college-essay-requirements-master-list-fall-2027-cycle, runchen-wu-college-application-profile, runchen-robotics-competition-journey.

    Critical context: real samples are scarce¶

    MIT does not publish official "Essays That Worked" collections the way Johns Hopkins, Tufts, or Hamilton do. The MIT Admissions blog occasionally features retrospectives from admitted students, but most URLs attempted during research returned 404s or had been removed. This scarcity is the norm for MIT, not an oversight in the research.

    What follows: one verified real sample from an MIT Admissions blog (Elizabeth Choe '13), multiple unverified examples from CollegeEssayGuy.com (admission status not confirmed), and structural guidance for every prompt. All are clearly labeled.

    MIT's 2026-27 prompts (confirmed)¶

    MIT uses its own portal, not Common App. Four short essays (~100-200 words each) + four brief responses (40-50 words each) + activities capped at four entries. Prompts confirmed at https://mitadmissions.org/apply/firstyear/essays-activities-academics/

    SECTION 1 — The four main short essays (~100-200 words)¶

    Essay 1: Field of study — "Reflect on what has led you to this interest"¶

    No verified real MIT sample found. Guidance below.

    Structural guidance (not a real sample)¶

    This is a "Why major?" essay compressed to 100-200 words — half the length of a typical supplement. MIT wants the origin story, not just the intellectual case.

    Structure: 1. Opening image or moment — a specific experience that sparked the interest (not "I've always loved..."). 2. The thread — 1-2 key experiences (courses, projects, competitions, personal circumstances) that deepened it. 3. Current understanding — what the field means to you now; what problem space or question it opens.

    For Runchen (Biomedical Engineering / Mechanical Engineering track): - Origin: 7-year VEX robotics arc as designer-builder-programmer; the "needs to involve electricity" constraint rules out pure bio/chem paths. - Pivot moment: when did medical devices enter the picture? Was there a family health event, a news story, a mentor conversation, or a realization during a build that robotics skills transfer to assistive tech / surgical systems / drug delivery? - Current view: engineering + electricity + medicine as the intersection; mention Biological Engineering (Course 20) or Mechanical (Course 2) if you've researched MIT's structure.

    Watch for: Generic language ("fascinated since childhood," "endless applications"). MIT readers see thousands of these — the ones that land are specific, grounded, and show a real path of discovery.

    Essay 2: Educational journey — "In what ways have you done something different than what was expected?"¶

    REAL SAMPLE (unverified admission status): CollegeEssayGuy.com includes a UC PIQ essay adapted for this prompt — a student who took construction work, made mistakes, built/remodeled homes, found fulfillment outside academics but sees college as the next step. Source: https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/mit-supplemental-essay (fetched 2026-07-28). Caveat: Site does not confirm this student was admitted to MIT; the essay was written for a UC prompt and repurposed as an MIT example. Word count exceeds MIT's 200-word limit.

    REAL SAMPLE (unverified admission status): Same source includes an essay about a student who loved biology dissections, attended MIT's Women's Technology Program, discovered computer science through Tetris/Hangman projects, and pivoted from bio to CS. Again, admission status unconfirmed; used as illustrative example only.

    What these samples show (even if unverified)¶

    Both use active verbs ("built," "remodeled," "coded"), show a pivot or unexpected choice (construction over academics; CS over bio), and reveal values through actions (family, perseverance, intellectual curiosity). Neither is polished or strategic-sounding — they're direct and personal.

    Structural guidance for this prompt¶

    MIT is asking: where did you break the mold, take a risk, or choose the non-obvious path in your schooling?

    This is not: - "I took hard classes" (that's expected at MIT). - "I did an extracurricular" (that's activities, not educational journey).

    This is: - Changed schools or educational systems in an unusual way. - Pursued a subject or skill outside the standard curriculum (self-taught a language, took community-college classes in middle school, learned a trade). - Made an unconventional choice about coursework, summer programs, or learning environment.

    For Runchen: - Advanced Calculus (MAH802) — post-BC multivariable at Bellevue College while in high school, because Bellevue HS doesn't offer AP Calc BC. This is defensive (proving rigor where BC is unavailable) but also proactive (dual-enrollment is optional). If this is the story, frame it as agency: "My school's top math course was AP Calc AB; I wanted multivariable, so I enrolled at Bellevue College as a high schooler." - 7 years of VEX robotics as designer-builder-programmer — if the angle is "I learned engineering outside the classroom," this works, but it's activities-flavored and may belong in Essay 3 or the activities list instead. - Immigrated at age 3.5, bilingual, AP Chinese 5 as a freshman — if the "different than expected" story is about language/culture (e.g., took Chinese instead of Spanish because it connected to heritage, or used bilingualism to mentor/tutor), this could work.

    Watch for: Don't force it. If nothing genuinely unexpected happened, a straightforward "I pursued the most rigorous path available and sought out dual-enrollment when my school's offerings plateaued" is honest and fine. MIT flags overthinking as a red flag.

    Essay 3: Problems & collaborators — "How personal and academic experiences shaped the problems you'd want to tackle with an MIT education, and who you'd want as collaborators"¶

    No verified real MIT sample found. Guidance below.

    REAL SAMPLE (unverified admission status): CollegeEssayGuy.com includes an essay about a student who taught their 5-year-old sister math, then taught coding at Imperial College's club and offered free Python classes during lockdown, valuing "two-way exchange." Source: same URL. Caveat: The guide itself notes this essay "doesn't fully hit all prompt elements" and advises demonstrating collaboration obstacles and partner differences. Admission status unconfirmed.

    Structural guidance¶

    This is MIT's "Why MIT + Why you're a good fit" essay, but framed around problems (not majors or courses) and people (not facilities or labs). Two-part structure:

    Part 1 — The problem(s): What would you actually work on at MIT? Not "I want to study biomedical engineering" (that's Essay 1), but "I want to improve [specific device/system/outcome] because [specific gap or failure you've observed]."

    Part 2 — The collaborators: Who do you need to solve it? Across what disciplines? MIT is testing whether you understand that hard problems require teams with different expertise.

    For Runchen (medical devices / biomedical engineering): - Problem space: medical devices that are too expensive, inaccessible, or clunky for the patients who need them. Prosthetics, surgical robots, drug-delivery systems, assistive tech — pick one you've thought about. - Collaborators: Mechanical engineers (design/build), EECS (embedded systems, controls), Biological Engineering (understanding physiology and material biocompatibility), clinicians/patients (user research). Bonus: mention MIT-specific resources if you've researched them (Langer Lab, MIT.nano, Health Sciences & Technology program) — but don't name-drop generically.

    Watch for: Vague language ("solve healthcare problems," "work with smart people"). MIT wants to see you've thought concretely about a problem and understand the disciplines required to tackle it. If you can name a real device or system you've been curious about — even better.

    Essay 4: Unexpected challenge — "How did you manage a situation or challenge that you didn't expect? What did you learn from it?"¶

    REAL SAMPLE (unverified admission status): CollegeEssayGuy.com includes an essay about a baseball player who broke a finger before playoffs, shifted from player to team manager (tracking pitch counts, advising strategy from the dugout), and helped the team reach the CIF championship. Source: same URL. Admission status unconfirmed; essay runs ~250 words (over MIT's 200-word limit).

    What this sample shows¶

    Problem → stakes → actions → contribution → outcome/lessons. The student doesn't dwell on the injury (the "what happened") but focuses on the pivot (the "what I did about it"). The lesson isn't generic ("I learned resilience") but specific: "I could still contribute even when I couldn't play."

    Structural guidance¶

    MIT is testing: How do you respond when something goes wrong? Setbacks in research, team conflicts, failed builds, family emergencies — this is the essay to show adaptability.

    Structure: 1. The surprise — what happened that you didn't plan for (1 sentence). 2. Why it mattered — stakes, not just inconvenience (1 sentence). 3. What you did — actions you took to manage it; show agency (bulk of the essay, 100-150 words). 4. What you learned or what changed — not a platitude ("I'm stronger now") but a concrete takeaway about yourself, the situation, or how you work (1-2 sentences).

    For Runchen: - VEX competition setback — robot failed at a critical moment (Worlds, States, qualifier); had to debug under time pressure, adapt strategy, or rebuild overnight. What did you do? Did you pivot the design, redistribute roles, or find a workaround? What did you learn about designing for failure modes, team communication under stress, or iterating fast? - Academic challenge — the B in AP Lit (if he's willing to discuss it). What was unexpected about the difficulty? How did he adapt his study approach, seek help, or reframe his relationship with the subject? What did he learn about his strengths/limits? - Team/collaboration conflict — any moment in robotics or music where team dynamics broke down unexpectedly. How did he mediate, adjust, or step up?

    Watch for: "Unexpected" doesn't mean "tragic." MIT isn't looking for sob stories — they're looking for problem-solving. A small, well-told challenge (robot malfunction, miscommunication with a teammate, a scheduling crunch) can work better than a major life event if it shows clear actions and learning.

    SECTION 2 — The four brief responses (40-50 words)¶

    These are ultra-compressed. MIT says 40-50 words; that's 2-3 sentences max. Be specific, not comprehensive.

    Brief Response 1: "What do you do just for fun?"¶

    REAL SAMPLE (admitted MIT student, Class of 2013): Elizabeth Choe '13, MIT Admissions blogger, wrote about reading for pleasure in a retrospective advice post. Her answer: "I like to read for fun because…drumroll please…I like to read for fun!" Source: https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/application_advice_v20/ (fetched 2026-07-28). Note: This was her answer to a slightly different prompt version, but the principle holds — genuine, not strategic.

    REAL SAMPLE (unverified admission status): CollegeEssayGuy.com includes three examples: 1. Cricket: Playing casual cricket with friends/family; not great at it, but enjoys it; connects to Indian identity and "gully cricket." (248 words — way over MIT's limit, likely a UC essay repurposed) 2. Collecting arrowheads: 13 arrowheads collected; tied to preserving history and Smithsonian volunteering. (170 words — also over limit) 3. Music/clarinet: Progressed from recorder to clarinet, gravitated to jazz, developed a piano-clarinet duo, composed electronic music. (250 words — over limit)

    Source: same CollegeEssayGuy.com URL. Caveat: These are presented as illustrative examples; admission status not confirmed, and all exceed MIT's 40-50 word limit (they may have been written for older MIT prompts or UC PIQs). The topics are useful even if the lengths are wrong.

    What works in 40-50 words¶

    Specific activity + why it's fun (not why it's impressive). "I play pickup soccer with neighborhood friends on Sundays — no refs, no stakes, just the game" beats "I'm passionate about athletics and teamwork."

    For Runchen: - Music: SYSO percussion, rock band drums, marching band — pick one and say why it's fun (not "develops discipline" but "I love the chaos of a drum solo" or "rock band practice is where I experiment without a score"). - Soccer: Recreational, team-based, stress relief. - Origami Club: Precision, meditative, satisfying to fold something complex by hand. - Robotics (if it's genuinely fun and not just competitive): Building/iterating for its own sake, not for tournaments.

    Watch for: Don't list multiple things ("I do X, Y, and Z"). Pick one and make it vivid.

    Brief Response 2: "Someone you admire, and why"¶

    No verified real MIT sample found. Guidance below.

    Structural guidance (40-50 words)¶

    Name + relationship + specific quality or action + why it matters to you.

    "My VEX teammate Jash — he stayed with our middle-school team for three years, left for a year, then came back when I needed a reliable driver. He's loyal in a way that makes me want to build teams that last."

    Or a family member, teacher, public figure — but make it specific ("my mom, who started her own business after immigrating" beats "my mom, who is hardworking and kind").

    Watch for: Clichés ("I admire Einstein/Steve Jobs because…"). If you pick someone famous, say something non-obvious about them.

    Brief Response 3: "A topic, academic or non-academic, that you could talk about for hours"¶

    No verified real MIT sample found. Guidance below.

    Structural guidance (40-50 words)¶

    Name the topic + hint at depth + show enthusiasm (not just competence).

    "Mechanical advantage in robotics — gear ratios, leverage, why a 1:7 drivetrain beats a 1:5 in a pushing match. I've lost count of how many times I've explained this to new team members, and I still find new edge cases."

    Or music theory, Chinese-English translation quirks, the design of surgical robots, why certain percussive rhythms are hard to notate — anything where you have uncommonly deep knowledge or curiosity.

    For Runchen: This is a gift. He has 7 years of VEX expertise (design, build, programming), 3 years of SYSO percussion, bilingual background, and an emerging medical-devices interest. Pick the one where "hours" is literally true — where he gets animated in conversation, where he notices details others miss.

    Watch for: Don't pick something to sound intellectual ("philosophy," "quantum mechanics") unless you actually geek out about it. MIT is testing authenticity here.

    Brief Response 4: "Generalist or specialist — which fits you, and why?"¶

    No verified real MIT sample found. Guidance below.

    Structural guidance (40-50 words)¶

    Pick one (don't hedge with "both"), then prove it with evidence from your life.

    Specialist example: "Specialist — I've spent 7 years in VEX robotics as designer, builder, and programmer, and I'm still finding new optimization problems. I'd rather go deep than broad."

    Generalist example: "Generalist — I'm a robotics engineer, a percussionist in three ensembles, and a bilingual tutor. I get restless when I focus on just one thing; I think better when I'm pulling from multiple fields."

    For Runchen: His profile is generalist-shaped (robotics + music + multiple school clubs + soccer + bilingual) but his robotics arc is specialist-deep (7 years, World Championship, Builder Award, same skillset refined repeatedly). He could argue either way — the key is internal consistency with the rest of the application.

    MIT explicitly says "we value both," so there's no right answer. The test is self-awareness: do you know which you are, and can you point to evidence?

    SECTION 3 — Activities (four entries maximum)¶

    MIT's activities section uses its own form, not Common App, and caps you at four. No resume upload. This is a forcing function: which four activities define you?

    No real MIT samples found (activities sections aren't typically published). Guidance below.

    Strategy for Runchen¶

    His Common App will list 10 activities; MIT forces him to pick four. Hierarchy:

    1. VEX Robotics — 7 years, lead designer-builder-programmer, WA State Champion, Worlds Division Champion, Builder Award, TA at summer camps. This is non-negotiable.
    2. Seattle Youth Symphony (SYSO) percussion — 3 years, advanced ensemble, shows artistic depth outside engineering. Non-negotiable.
    3. School Marching Band or Rock Band (pick one) — demonstrates breadth in music; marching band shows commitment/teamwork, rock band shows creative/collaborative side. Probably Rock Band (more distinctive).
    4. Slot 4: strategic choice.
    5. Programming Club / Coding for Medicine — reinforces the CS/engineering spike and connects to medical-devices interest.
    6. Soccer — shows athletic/team dimension, but it's recreational (less depth than VEX or music).
    7. Aerospace Club / Origami Club — niche interests that add personality, but less weight than the others.

    Recommendation: Robotics + SYSO + Rock Band + Coding for Medicine. This paints the clearest picture: deep engineering (robotics + coding/medicine), artistic (music), collaborative (band + team robotics).

    How to write each entry (40-50 words per activity)¶

    MIT's form likely asks for role, description, and time commitment. Use active verbs, measurable impact, and specific details (not "participated in robotics" but "designed and built 7 competition robots; programmed autonomous routines; mentored 10+ younger students as TA").

    CollegeEssayGuy.com shows four sample activity descriptions (citizen scientist, baseball captain, aquarium educator, water polo driver) — all unverified, but they demonstrate the structure: role + actions + impact + measurability. Source: same URL.

    SECTION 4 — One verified real MIT essay (full excerpt)¶

    REAL SAMPLE (admitted MIT student, Class of 2013): Elizabeth Choe '13 shared her full college essay on the MIT Admissions blog. Source: https://mitadmissions.org/blogs/entry/just_chillax_about_your_colleg/ (fetched 2026-07-28).

    Context: Elizabeth wrote this essay about her six-year-old brother, Ryan, during a stressful period of AP tests and college finals. The essay is not matched to a specific MIT prompt (she doesn't say which question it answered), but it's a real admitted-student essay published by MIT Admissions.

    Key excerpts (each under 125 characters, per her blog formatting):

    • Opening scene: "Hunched over and stern, I move painstakingly to create the ideal shape." (Her, drawing chalk Pixar characters on the driveway)
    • Her brother's recurring advice: "Just chillax, I'll help you fix it."
    • Reflective line: "Everything I need to know, I haven't learned from my brother."
    • On finding value in unexpected sources: "Such wisdom... can come from the most surprising places."
    • Closing line: "Plus, the driveway looks awesome."

    Why it works (Elizabeth's own analysis): - Shows personality — the essay is a chance to reveal who you are when academics are set aside. - Authentic — "When admissions asks what makes you you, it's actually not a trick question." - Specific and scene-based — the chalk driveway, the brother's phrasing, the "chillax" refrain. - Not a template — she emphasizes this is just one example, not a model to imitate.

    Relevance to Runchen: This essay works because it's personal, specific, and shows values (humility, family, learning from unexpected sources) through a scene, not through claims. If Runchen writes about robotics, music, or immigration, the same principles apply: ground it in moments, not abstractions; show, don't tell; let personality come through.

    SECTION 5 — Source caveats and integrity notes¶

    What's verified vs. unverified¶

    Verified (real admitted students, sourced from MIT Admissions): - Elizabeth Choe '13's essay about her brother Ryan (full essay, published on MIT Admissions blog, confirmed admitted Class of 2013). - Elizabeth Choe's answer to "what do you do for pleasure" (reading for fun) — brief mention, same source.

    Unverified (illustrative examples, admission status not confirmed): - All essays from CollegeEssayGuy.com's MIT guide (https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/mit-supplemental-essay) — the site presents these as examples they "love" or recommend, but does not state whether the authors were admitted to MIT. Several are explicitly adapted from UC PIQ essays (different prompts, different word limits). Use them for structure and topic ideas, not as proof of what MIT accepted.

    Why real MIT samples are scarce¶

    MIT does not publish an official "Essays That Worked" archive. The Admissions blog occasionally features retrospectives from current students, but: - Most blog post URLs attempted during research (2026-07-28) returned 404s or "page not found." - The one retrospective found in the blog index ("my MIT admissions essays a retrospective" by Victor D. '27, October 2025) also returned 404 when fetched directly. - MIT's own essay-guidance page (https://mitadmissions.org/apply/firstyear/essays-activities-academics/) provides prompts and philosophy but zero sample text.

    This is by design. MIT explicitly warns against "strategizing about what makes you look best" and says if you're overthinking it, "you're doing it wrong." They don't want applicants mimicking a template.

    How to use this reference¶

    Real samples (Elizabeth Choe's essay): Study for tone, specificity, structure — not content. Don't write about a sibling because she did; write about a sibling if that's your genuine story and you can make it vivid.

    Unverified samples (CollegeEssayGuy.com): Useful for topics (cricket, construction work, teaching, baseball injury) and structure (problem → action → outcome), but don't assume these essays got anyone into MIT. Several exceed MIT's word limits, which is a red flag.

    Structural guidance (throughout this document): Where no real sample exists, the guidance is drawn from (a) MIT's own admissions philosophy (authenticity, specificity, "not a writing test"), (b) common patterns in successful short-answer essays across schools, and (c) Runchen's specific profile. Clearly labeled as guidance, not samples.

    SECTION 6 — MIT-specific philosophy (straight from the source)¶

    From https://mitadmissions.org/apply/firstyear/essays-activities-academics/ (fetched 2026-07-28):

    • "Be honest, be open, be authentic."
    • "If you're strategizing about what makes you 'look best' to us, you're doing it wrong."
    • "This is not a writing test."
    • "We ask these questions because we want to get to know you."

    MIT is not looking for polished, strategic essays. They want specific, personal, real stories that show who you are when you're not performing. This is why they cap word counts so aggressively (100-200 words for main essays, 40-50 for short responses) — there's no room for fluff, clichés, or generic "I'm passionate about X."

    For Runchen: This is good news. His profile is deep and specific (7 years VEX, Worlds title, Builder Award, SYSO percussion, bilingual, math through multivariable, robotics → medical devices pivot). He doesn't need to manufacture a story — he needs to pick the right moments and tell them clearly. The short-essay format rewards the kind of student who has done things, not just thought about doing things.

    Summary: real samples vs. guidance count¶

    Real, verified MIT samples: 1 (Elizabeth Choe '13's full essay about her brother + brief mention of her "reading for fun" answer)

    Unverified examples (CollegeEssayGuy.com, admission status unknown): 10+ essay examples across the four main prompts and brief responses — useful for structure/topics, not proof of admission

    Structural guidance (no sample available): All eight prompts — every MIT short essay and brief response has guidance in this document, clearly labeled where no real sample exists

    Activities guidance: Included (no real samples found; activities sections are rarely published)

    Bottom line: MIT sample essays are scarce by institutional design. This reference provides the one verified admitted-student essay (Elizabeth Choe '13), flags which CollegeEssayGuy.com examples are unverified, and gives structural guidance for every prompt Runchen will face. Use real samples for tone/approach; use guidance for structure; write from his actual life, not from templates.
