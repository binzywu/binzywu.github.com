    Penn (UPenn) Sample Essays Reference (Fall 2027)

    Reference guide for Penn supplemental essay writing, compiled for Runchen Wu's Fall 2027 application cycle. See university-of-pennsylvania-college-research, runchen-college-essay-requirements-master-list-fall-2027-cycle, runchen-robotics-competition-journey, and his complete application profile.

    Critical finding: Real Penn samples are not publicly available¶

    Penn does not maintain a public "Essays That Worked" collection like Johns Hopkins, Tufts, Hamilton, or Connecticut College. Extensive research (2026-07-28) across Daily Pennsylvanian archives, CollegeEssayGuy, PrepScholar, AdmitSee, IvyWise, Empowerly, Reddit's ApplyingToCollege, EssayForum, and other essay repositories found zero verified admitted-student Penn supplemental essays in the public domain.

    The only examples located were hypothetical teaching samples authored by college consulting firms (primarily CollegeVine), clearly labeled as instructional examples rather than actual admitted-student work. These are useful for understanding structure and approach, but they are not real essays.

    Integrity note: Following the research protocol in /tmp/runchen_profile.txt, this document will NOT fabricate essays or present hypothetical examples as real admitted-student work. Where no real sample exists, structural guidance and annotated outlines are provided instead, clearly labeled as guidance.

    Penn's 2025-26 supplemental prompts (verify for Fall 2027 cycle)¶

    Per runchen-college-essay-requirements-master-list-fall-2027-cycle, Penn requires three supplemental essays for all applicants, plus one school-specific prompt. These are 2025-26 prompts and must be re-verified after August 1, 2026 when Penn releases Fall 2027 requirements.

    # Prompt Words
    1 Write a short thank-you note to someone you have not yet thanked and would like to acknowledge 150-200
    2 How will you explore community at Penn? 150-200
    3 School-specific (Engineering/SEAS wording not published; historically asks how you plan to pursue your interests at Penn Engineering) 150-200

    Plus the Common App 650-word personal essay (required for all schools except UCs and MIT on Runchen's list).

    What Penn values in supplemental essays (official guidance)¶

    From Penn Admissions (via Empowerly research, 2026-07-28):

    • "We read your words carefully, as they are yet another window into how you think, what you value, and how you will contribute to and benefit from Penn."
    • "This is your story: your experiences, your ideas, your perspective."

    Translation: Penn wants authentic voice, demonstrated knowledge of Penn-specific resources, and genuine connection between your background and what Penn offers — not generic Ivy prestige statements.

    Prompt 1 — Thank-you note (150-200 words)¶

    What the prompt asks¶

    Write a short thank-you note to someone you have not yet thanked and would like to acknowledge.

    Why Penn asks this¶

    Surface-level read: gratitude and self-awareness. Deeper read: can you write with genuine emotion, specificity, and humility? The constraint — someone you haven't yet thanked — prevents recycling a teacher recommendation or predictable parent tribute. Penn wants to see who shaped you in ways you haven't already documented elsewhere in the application.

    No real samples available — Structural guidance instead¶

    Because no authenticated admitted-student examples exist in public sources, here is annotated structural guidance for this prompt type:

    Structure that works: 1. Opening sentence: name the person and the un-thanked moment/gift (1 sentence) - Not "Dear Mr. Smith, thank you for everything" — too generic - Instead: "Mr. Rodriguez, I never thanked you for staying two hours after school that Friday in March when my robot's autonomous code kept failing and States was three days away."

    1. What they gave you that you didn't see at the time (2-3 sentences)
    2. The specific skill, mindset, or realization their action unlocked

    3. Connect to a concrete before/after: "Before that session, I thought debugging meant rewriting from scratch. You taught me to trace logic backward, line by line — a method I've used in every build since."

    4. Why it mattered beyond that moment (2-3 sentences)

    5. How it shaped your approach, character, or trajectory

    6. Avoid platitudes ("you inspired me"); show the specific change

    7. Closing: the thank-you itself (1 sentence)

    8. Warm, direct, personal
    9. "Thank you for teaching me that patience is an engineering skill, not just a virtue."

    Word count: 150-200 forces brutal specificity. Every sentence must carry weight. No room for filler or abstract praise.

    Runchen-specific angles: - VEX mentor/coach who taught a non-obvious lesson — not "thank you for coaching us to Worlds," but something quieter: the moment they taught him to rebuild a failed mechanism instead of abandoning it, or how they modeled calm under pressure during a competition malfunction - Blaze Robotics teammate (Jash or another) who stuck with the team through the move, teaching him about loyalty or collaboration when it would've been easier to quit - SYSO section leader or conductor who gave feedback on a difficult piece that changed how he approaches practice - Family member (not parent) — extended family, sibling, grandparent — who shaped his immigrant/bilingual identity or work ethic in a way he took for granted

    What to avoid: - Generic teacher praise ("you made class fun") - Parent tributes (too expected; save for other essays if needed) - Thanking someone for an achievement you already listed (that's redundant) - Vague gratitude without specific detail

    Prompt 2 — Community at Penn (150-200 words)¶

    What the prompt asks¶

    How will you explore community at Penn?

    Why Penn asks this¶

    Penn's campus culture emphasizes residential college life, pre-professional clubs, interdisciplinary collaboration, and Philadelphia civic engagement. This prompt tests: (1) Do you know what Penn's community actually consists of beyond "smart Ivy students"? (2) Can you articulate how you'll actively contribute, not just benefit? (3) Do you see community as something you shape, not something that passively happens to you?

    No real samples available — Structural guidance instead¶

    Structure that works: 1. Lead with a specific Penn community you're drawn to (1-2 sentences) - Name the actual organization, space, or tradition — not "Penn's diverse community" - Examples: Penn Electric Racing, GRASP Lab open houses, Quadramics (a cappella), Civic House programs, a specific residential program

    1. Connect it to your background/spike (2-3 sentences)
    2. What you bring: "Seven years building competitive robots taught me that the best designs come from teammates who challenge each other's assumptions — I'm drawn to Penn Electric Racing's collaborative build model because..."

    3. What you'll learn: "...and I want to learn how senior members integrate electrical, mechanical, and aerodynamics expertise into a single vehicle."

    4. Add a second community layer if word count allows (1-2 sentences)

    5. Shows breadth: maybe SEAS community + a music ensemble (SYSO background) or a cultural/identity org

    6. Or: campus community + Philadelphia community (Civic House, local robotics mentorship)

    7. Close with what you'll contribute (1 sentence)

    8. Active voice: "I'll bring my VEX teaching experience to Penn Robotics outreach in West Philly schools."
    9. Not passive: "I hope to maybe join some clubs."

    Runchen-specific angles: - Penn Electric Racing or Penn Robotics Club — ties directly to his 7-year VEX spike and World Championship experience; he can speak credibly about build culture and team dynamics - GRASP Lab (General Robotics, Automation, Sensing and Perception) — world-class robotics research, undergraduate involvement possible; shows he's researched beyond undergrad clubs - SYSO → Penn music ensembles (Penn Symphony Orchestra, Penn Band, or smaller chamber/percussion groups) — demonstrates he'll continue music as part of his identity, not abandon it for engineering-only focus - Civic House or Engineers Without Borders — if he wants to connect his medical-devices interest to community impact/access - Penn's residential college system — avoid generic "I'll make friends in my dorm"; instead name a specific house program (e.g., Gregory College House's emphasis on community engagement, or Rodin's Arts emphasis if relevant)

    What to avoid: - Listing clubs without showing why they fit you specifically - Generic "diverse perspectives" language with no concrete Penn references - Only taking (learning, growing) without giving (contributing, building, teaching) - Mentioning clubs/orgs that don't exist at Penn (verify names on Penn's website)

    Prompt 3 — School-specific: Engineering (SEAS) (150-200 words)¶

    What the prompt asks¶

    Engineering-specific wording not published on Penn's admissions site for 2025-26 cycle. Historically, Penn Engineering has asked: "How do you plan to pursue your engineering interests at Penn?"

    Must re-verify prompt wording for Fall 2027 cycle after August 1, 2026.

    Why Penn asks this¶

    SEAS wants to know: (1) Do you understand what specific engineering resources Penn offers (labs, faculty, programs, interdisciplinary options)? (2) Does your academic/career trajectory align with what Penn Engineering actually provides? (3) Are you applying to Penn Engineering because you researched it, or because "Ivy League engineering" sounds good?

    Penn Engineering's differentiators: GRASP Lab (robotics), top-10 bioengineering, interdisciplinary programs (M&T, VIPER, Digital Media Design), strong medical device / Penn Medicine ties, integrated product design, emphasis on entrepreneurship/impact.

    No real samples available — Structural guidance instead¶

    Structure that works: 1. Lead with your engineering interest and why it matters to you (2-3 sentences) - Start specific, not broad: "Seven years of competitive robotics taught me that the best machines balance mechanical precision with elegant code — but I'm pivoting toward medical devices because..." - Include the "why": personal story, observed problem, or intellectual curiosity

    1. Name 2-3 specific Penn Engineering resources that align (3-4 sentences)
    2. Be specific: Professor names, lab names, course titles, programs
    3. Show fit: Explain why each matters to your trajectory

    4. Example: "Professor Daniel Lee's work in GRASP Lab on autonomous surgical robots directly bridges my robotics background with my medical-device goals. I'm also drawn to Penn's Integrated Product Design program, which teaches engineers to prototype with human-centered design..."

    5. Close with impact or contribution (1-2 sentences)

    6. What you'll build, research, or create
    7. "I want to design assistive devices that combine precision robotics with accessibility — the kind of medical technology that improves daily life, not just saves it."

    Runchen-specific angles: - GRASP Lab — world-class robotics research; he has 7 years VEX + World Championship credibility to speak about robotics with authority - Bioengineering / medical devices — Penn Bioengineering is top-10 nationally; strong ties to Penn Medicine (one of the nation's top hospitals); research in prosthetics, surgical robotics, neural interfaces, drug delivery - Integrated Product Design (IPD) — interdisciplinary program combining engineering, design, and business; great for students interested in product development and entrepreneurship - M&T Program (Management & Technology) — if he wants to explore business/entrepreneurship side (probably not, given his "needs to involve electricity" preference, but worth knowing) - Specific faculty — research Penn Engineering faculty in robotics, BME, or electrical engineering whose work aligns with his interests; mentioning 1-2 by name shows deep research - Penn Medicine proximity — if he's serious about medical devices, Penn's hospital and med school adjacency is a massive advantage for clinical exposure and design feedback

    What to avoid: - Generic "Penn has great engineering" without specifics - Listing random courses or majors without connecting them to his story - Overstating interest in a program he hasn't researched (e.g., claiming deep passion for neural engineering if his background is pure mechanical robotics) - Ignoring his robotics spike — it's his strongest asset and Penn has elite robotics resources

    Common App personal essay — not Penn-specific, but relevant¶

    The 650-word Common App personal essay is required for Penn (and nearly every school on Runchen's list except UCs, Cal Poly, and MIT). It's not Penn-specific, but Penn will read it as the longest writing sample in his application.

    Where his Penn supplements should connect to it: - If the Common App essay is about robotics (likely his strongest narrative), the Penn supplements should add new dimensions — music, teaching, community, gratitude — rather than repeating the same VEX story - If the Common App essay covers a different topic (immigration, family, identity, music), then Penn Prompt 3 (Engineering) is his best place to showcase his robotics spike in detail

    Strategy note: Penn reads all four essays (Common App + 3 Penn supplements) together. They should feel like a cohesive portrait, not four disconnected pieces. Avoid redundancy; maximize what Penn learns about him across all 800 words of Penn writing (650 Common App + 150-200 × 3 Penn supplements = ~1,250 words total).
