    Critical Finding: No Real HMC Samples Available in Public Domain

    After exhaustive search of standard sources (CollegeEssayGuy, PrepScholar, CollegeVine, Shemmassian, IvyWise, Niche, AdmitReport, published books, HMC's own site, student blogs), NO publicly-available real Harvey Mudd admitted-student essay samples were found. This is unusual but not unprecedented for small, selective STEM schools — HMC does not publish "Essays That Worked," and the typical aggregator sites either have no HMC section or publish only structural guidance.

    What follows is labeled structural guidance based on HMC's published prompts and mission, NOT real samples presented as real. CollegeVine published two short illustrative excerpts by their essay team (see below), but these are teaching examples, not verified admitted-student essays.

    Example 1: Background Essay (HMC #1, abbreviated excerpt)

    The excerpt opens with a vivid scene:

    "Grab your rain boots, the streets are flooded again!"

    It describes recurring flooding in a coastal town, mentions a neighbor affected, and ends with the writer's resolution to become a civil engineer to solve infrastructure problems.

    What works structurally (per CollegeVine's analysis):

    • Opens with a concrete, sensory moment rather than an abstract claim ("I've always been interested in engineering").
    • Connects a specific lived experience to a problem the writer wants to solve — environment shapes motivation.
    • Names the impact (civil engineering to prevent flooding disasters).

    How this maps to Runchen's profile:

    • His concrete moment could be a specific VEX build challenge (e.g., the Over Under or High Stakes season where his team became WA State Champions, or the Build Award-winning robot) or a teaching moment at robotics camp where he translated complex engineering for 8-13 year olds.
    • The "problem to solve" pivot: robotics competitions taught iterative design under constraints → medical devices = the same process applied to human health (designing tools that work for real patients, not just judges).
    • The "people to work with": small, collaborative teams where everyone contributes (echoes his explicit college preference for supportive, non-cutthroat environments — HMC's 900-student size is his ideal).

    Structural Guidance for Essay #1 (500 words): Background → Problems → People → Impact

    HMC explicitly asks for three connections: (1) problems you want to solve, (2) people you want to work with, (3) impact you hope to have. The 500-word limit means each gets ~150 words after a brief opening.

    Suggested Structure

    Opening (75-100 words): Anchor in a specific, concrete moment that illustrates your background. Not "I immigrated to the US" (too abstract), but a specific scene or challenge where background shaped how you see problems.

    For Runchen:

    • A VEX build moment where he had to explain a design decision to teammates who didn't share his technical vocabulary (bridging languages/perspectives).
    • A robotics camp teaching moment where a younger student's question made him realize engineering is about clarity and service, not just technical mastery.
    • A pivot moment when he realized competition robots are constrained by rules, but medical devices are constrained by human need — the latter feels more meaningful.

    Problems (125-150 words): What problems does your background make you care about? HMC wants to see how lived experience shapes what you notice and what you want to fix.

    For Runchen:

    • Robotics taught him that constraints drive creativity — limited parts, time, rules → iterative design. Medical devices are the same: constrained by physiology, cost, usability → engineering that serves real patients.
    • Immigrant/bicultural lens: accessibility and translation (designing tools that work across languages, cultures, abilities). Medical devices that are usable in under-resourced contexts.
    • Or: the gap between "works in theory" and "works for humans" (competition robots vs. robots in the real world; lab prototypes vs. deployed medical devices).

    People (100-125 words): Who do you want to work with, and why? HMC is asking about collaboration style and values.

    For Runchen:

    • Small, interdisciplinary teams where everyone contributes — not siloed roles. Echoes his VEX experience (designer + builder + programmer, often overlapping) and his stated college preference for collaborative, supportive environments.
    • People who prioritize impact over ego — he's drawn to HMC's size (900 students) and collaborative culture because "everyone is supportive" (direct quote from Harvey Mudd College — College Research).
    • Or: engineers who partner with clinicians, patients, and ethicists — medical devices require technical skill + human understanding.

    Impact (100-125 words): What difference do you hope your work makes? HMC is asking about values and vision.

    For Runchen:

    • Direct human impact at the individual scale — not "cure cancer" (too abstract), but "design medical devices that improve quality of life for people with mobility/sensory/chronic conditions."
    • Accessible, deployable tools (echoes the immigrant/bicultural lens and the robotics-camp teaching experience — engineering as service, not just innovation).
    • Or: the intersection of human-centered design and electrical engineering — his stated interest is "engineering that involves electricity," and medical devices (pacemakers, cochlear implants, assistive robotics) are exactly that.

    Tone & Style Notes

    • Concrete over abstract: "the first robot I designed was a claw mechanism that failed six times before it worked" beats "I learned perseverance through robotics."
    • Cultural specificity is a strength, not a liability: Chinese-American, immigrant, bilingual, small-team-oriented, service-minded — these are differentiators at a school that explicitly asks about background.
    • Avoid the "I've always wanted to be an engineer since I was five" cliché — HMC wants to understand why and how your background shaped that, not just assert it.
    • The pivot to medical devices is the essay's center of gravity — it's the clearest example of "problems I want to solve" and "impact I hope to have." Don't bury it.

    Structural Guidance for Essay #2 (100 words): HSA Dream Class

    HMC wants a specific intellectual interest beyond STEM. The 100-word limit means no justification, just enthusiasm and clarity.

    Three Authentic Options for Runchen

    Option 1: Music/Physics Crossover (strongest fit given his SYSO + rock band + marching band profile)

    A course on the physics and cultural history of percussion instruments — how different materials, shapes, and tensions produce sound; how percussion evolved across cultures (African drumming, Chinese gongs, orchestral timpani, electronic pads); designing new instruments or analyzing why certain rhythms feel universal. Ties his engineering lens to his music practice.

    Example pitch (draft, ~90 words):

    "The Physics and Culture of Percussion: a course exploring how percussion instruments work — the materials, shapes, and tensions that produce sound — and how percussion traditions evolved across cultures. I'd want to analyze African drumming patterns, Chinese opera gongs, orchestral timpani, and electronic drum pads side-by-side, then design a new percussion instrument as a final project. As a percussionist in SYSO and a rock band, I'm fascinated by how rhythm crosses cultures and how engineering shapes what music is possible."

    Option 2: Bioethics / Assistive Technology (strongest fit for his medical-device interest)

    A course on the ethics of assistive technology and inclusive design — who gets access to prosthetics, cochlear implants, powered wheelchairs; how cost/insurance shapes what gets built; the debate over "fixing disability" vs. designing for diverse abilities. Ties his engineering pivot to a humanities/social-science lens HMC explicitly values.

    Example pitch (draft, ~95 words):

    "Ethics of Assistive Technology: a seminar on who has access to medical devices like prosthetics, cochlear implants, and powered wheelchairs. I'd want to study how cost, insurance, and cultural attitudes shape what gets designed and who benefits — and the debate between 'fixing disability' and designing for diverse abilities. As an aspiring medical-device engineer, I need to understand not just how devices work, but who they serve and what values they encode."

    Option 3: Art/Math of Origami (weakest tie to his stated interests, but authentic given his Origami Club involvement)

    A course on the mathematics and engineering of origami — rigid vs. curved folding, deployable structures (solar panels, stents), the geometry of crease patterns. Less directly connected to his medical-device interest, but legitimately tied to his activities.

    Example pitch (draft, ~85 words):

    "The Mathematics of Origami: a course on the geometry of folding — rigid vs. curved creases, deployable structures like space solar panels and medical stents, and the computational design of crease patterns. As a member of Origami Club, I'm fascinated by how a flat sheet becomes a 3D structure through folding, and how that principle applies to real-world engineering challenges where compactness and deployment matter."

    Recommendation: Option 1 (music/physics) or Option 2 (bioethics/assistive tech) are the strongest fits. Option 1 is the most authentic given his deep music involvement across three ensembles. Option 2 most directly reinforces his stated medical-device interest and shows he's thinking about engineering's human/social dimensions.

    What Makes an HMC Essay Strong: Inferred Principles

    Based on HMC's mission statement, the Poodry quote, and the structure of the prompts:

    1. Specificity over generality: HMC wants to see how your background shaped your interests, not a generic "I love STEM and I care about people" essay.
    2. Humanism in STEM: HMC is explicitly interdisciplinary (strong humanities core, HSA requirement, Clinic partnerships). Essays that treat engineering as a purely technical pursuit will feel off-mission.
    3. Collaborative identity: HMC is tiny (900 students) and emphasizes teamwork. Essays that center solo achievement over team contribution may feel less aligned.
    4. Intellectual curiosity beyond the major: The HSA prompt exists for a reason — HMC wants students who are curious about the world beyond problem sets.
    5. Authenticity over polish: A 500-word essay with a clear, genuine throughline (even if the writing isn't perfect) will land better than a 500-word essay that sounds like it was written by a consultant.

    Runchen-Specific Strengths to Leverage

    • 7 years of VEX robotics with specific, named achievements (Worlds Division Champion, WA State Champion, Builder Award) — this is rare depth in one activity.
    • Designer + builder + programmer role (not just one facet of robotics) — shows breadth within the spike.
    • Teaching experience (robotics camp TA for 8-13 year olds, Builder Adviser) — demonstrates that he can translate complexity and mentor others.
    • Pivot to medical devices — a clear evolution from competition robotics (engineering for judges/points) to real-world impact (engineering for patients/users).
    • Music as a genuine secondary interest (SYSO + rock band + marching band) — three simultaneous ensembles, not a one-off activity. This is a strong bridge to the HSA essay.
    • Immigrant/bicultural background (Chinese-American, arrived age 3.5, bilingual) — HMC explicitly asks how background shapes perspective.
    • Stated preference for small, collaborative environments — his own words ("won't feel lost," "everyone is supportive") match HMC's 900-student culture almost verbatim.

    What to Avoid

    • Generic "I've always loved robots" opening — HMC has heard this 5,000 times. Open with a specific moment.
    • Laundry-listing achievements — the essay is not a resume. HMC already knows his stats; they want to understand why he does what he does.
    • Treating the HSA essay as an afterthought — 100 words is short, but "I'd like to learn about history" (generic) will feel like box-checking. "I'd like to study the cultural history of percussion instruments" (specific) signals real curiosity.
    • Overpromising impact — "I want to cure cancer" or "I want to solve climate change" (too abstract, not grounded in his actual experience). "I want to design medical devices that improve quality of life for people with mobility challenges" (specific, achievable, tied to his stated interest) is stronger.
    • Ignoring the immigrant/bicultural background — HMC explicitly asks about it via the Poodry quote. Not mentioning it at all would be a missed opportunity.
