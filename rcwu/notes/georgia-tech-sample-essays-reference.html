    The Georgia Tech Prompt (300 words)

    "Why do you want to study your chosen major, and why do you want to study that major at Georgia Tech?"

    This single 300-word short answer is Georgia Tech's only supplement beyond the Common App 650-word personal essay. Georgia Tech uses the Common App exclusively. Their AI policy: brainstorming and editing are acceptable, but final submission must be the student's own work. (Source: runchen-college-essay-requirements-master-list-fall-2027-cycle)

    GT evaluation explicitly weighs "genuine interest in Georgia Tech" — this is not just a "Why Major" essay; the "Why Georgia Tech" half matters equally. Demonstrated interest counts.

    Section B: Where Real Samples Don't Exist — Structural Guidance

    Because real admitted-student essays specifically addressing the current "Why Major + Why Georgia Tech" prompt are not publicly available, the following are LABELED GUIDANCE STRUCTURES, NOT REAL ESSAYS.

    Approach 1: The "Problem-Solution-GT Resources" Structure

    Best for: Students with a clear problem they want to solve (matches Runchen's medical-devices interest)

    Structure (not a sample essay, guidance only):

    1. Opening: the problem that drives you (50–75 words)
    2. Specific moment or realization that sparked interest in the major
    3. For Runchen: VEX robotics competition → designing autonomous systems that react in real-time → curiosity about how human body's systems respond to medical interventions → medical devices as "robots for health"
    1. Why this major: skills + mindset developed (75–100 words)
    2. 7 years VEX: lead designer, builder, programmer → systems thinking, iteration under constraints, debugging failures
    3. World Championship Division Champion + Builder Award → validation of design approach
    4. "Needs to involve electricity" → BME as the intersection of robotics precision and human impact
    1. Why Georgia Tech: specific resources mapped to goals (100–125 words)
    2. Wallace H. Coulter Department of Biomedical Engineering (joint GT/Emory) — top-ranked, emphasizes translational research
    3. Name 2–3 specific GT BME faculty whose research aligns with robotics/medical devices (e.g., prosthetics, neural interfaces, surgical robotics, medical instrumentation)
    4. InVenture Prize (GT's student inventor competition) — direct parallel to VEX competitions, but solving real-world problems
    5. GT's maker spaces + project-based culture — hands-on engineering, not just theory
    1. Future-facing close (25–50 words)
    2. Building medical devices that improve lives, combining competition-robot precision with healthcare impact
    3. At GT, turning 7 years of "build-test-iterate" into devices that matter beyond the competition field

    Why this structure works for Runchen:

    • His robotics spike is the foundation, not an aside
    • Medical-devices pivot becomes a logical extension (autonomy + human systems), not a random shift
    • GT's BME pedigree + maker culture are natural fits, not generic praise
    • "Builder Award at Worlds" is evidence he already operates at GT's "creating the next" level

    Approach 2: The "Skill Thread Across Time" Structure

    Best for: Students who can show major-relevant skill development across multiple contexts

    Structure (guidance only):

    1. Skill introduction via specific moment (50 words)
    2. "I learned iteration matters more than perfection when our VEX robot failed 30 seconds into Worlds qualification"
    1. Skill applied in major-adjacent context (75 words)
    2. Redesigned subsystem overnight → Division Champion next day
    3. Same iteration mindset: teaching robotics summer camp (translate complex → approachable), SYSO percussion (precision timing in ensemble), rock band (adapt on the fly)
    1. Skill as major requirement (50 words)
    2. Biomedical engineering IS iteration: prototypes fail, human systems are unpredictable, FDA approval requires exhaustive testing
    3. 7 years of "build-break-rebuild" prepared me for this
    1. Skill + GT resources (100 words)
    2. GT's BME emphasizes translational research (lab → clinic), not just theory
    3. Name faculty, labs, project-based courses
    4. InVenture Prize, maker spaces, collaborative (not cutthroat) culture
    1. Close (25 words)
    2. At GT, iteration becomes innovation that improves lives

    Why this works for Runchen:

    • Single through-line (iteration) unifies robotics, teaching, music, and BME
    • VEX Worlds failure-to-Champion story is dramatic and real
    • Shows resilience, not just achievement

    4. Name faculty, specific labs/courses
    5. Collaborative culture + maker spaces

    Section D: Common Pitfalls to Avoid (per CollegeVine Analysis)

    CollegeVine's hypothetical weak vs. strong examples (https://blog.collegevine.com/how-to-write-the-georgia-tech-essays/) highlight these mistakes:

    ❌ Pitfall 1: Generic Major Interest

    Weak example: "I like to work with my hands and I do well in math and physics." Why it fails: Applies to any engineering major at any school; no specificity

    Fix for Runchen: Name VEX-specific moments — "When our robot's autonomous code failed 30 seconds into Worlds qualification, I had 8 hours to redesign the sensor array. We won Division Champion the next day."

    ❌ Pitfall 2: Praising GT's Rankings

    Weak example: "Georgia Tech is the second-best undergraduate civil engineering program." Why it fails: GT already knows its rankings; doesn't show research or genuine fit

    Fix for Runchen: Don't mention GT's BME ranking. Instead: "The Coulter Department's joint GT/Emory structure means undergrads work alongside med students and clinicians — the translational pipeline I want."

    ❌ Pitfall 3: Vague Resource Name-Dropping

    Weak example: "I look forward to joining Engineers Without Borders." Why it fails: Generic club; doesn't tie to student's specific interests

    Fix for Runchen: "The InVenture Prize mirrors VEX Worlds' pressure — design, build, defend under time constraints — but the outcome isn't trophies, it's patents and prototypes that reach patients."

    ❌ Pitfall 4: Future Without Specificity

    Weak example: "A degree from GT will allow me to further my passion and use my skills in my career." Why it fails: Says nothing; could be copy-pasted to any school/major

    Fix for Runchen: "At GT, I'll apply 7 years of build-test-iterate to medical devices — turning competition-robot precision into prosthetics, surgical tools, and health monitors that improve lives beyond the arena."

    Section F: Writing Process Recommendations

    Step 1: Research First, Draft Second

    Don't start writing until Runchen has:

    • Identified 2–3 GT BME faculty whose research connects to robotics/medical devices
    • Researched InVenture Prize, Invention Studio, CREATE-X
    • (If possible) Attended a GT virtual info session or visited campus

    Why: Both real samples show deep research. Fabricating GT "fit" without real knowledge reads as generic.

    Step 2: Lead with VEX, Pivot to Medical Devices

    Opening should NOT be: "I've always been interested in biomedical engineering." Opening SHOULD be: Specific VEX moment that illustrates design-build-program skill → transition to "but competition robots don't save lives, medical devices do."

    Why: His spike is robotics, not BME. Trying to hide that wastes his strongest asset. The pivot shows maturity.

    Step 3: Use Technical Language (But Not Jargon)

    Real Sample 2 uses "bandgaps," "methane clathrates," "Boltzmann and Saha Equations" — authentic technical interest.

    Runchen's equivalents from VEX:

    • Autonomous navigation algorithms
    • PID control loops
    • Sensor fusion (e.g., vision + inertial measurement)
    • Iterative CAD prototyping in Fusion 360
    • Real-time system debugging under competition pressure

    Use 1–2 technical terms naturally, not forced. Proves he's not generic "I like STEM" applicant.

    Step 4: Word-Count Budget (300 words total)

    Suggested allocation:

    • Why Major (origin story + skills): 100–125 words
    • Why GT (resources + fit): 150–175 words
    • Future-facing close: 25–50 words

    Critical: GT weighs "genuine interest in Georgia Tech" explicitly. The "Why GT" section must be longer and more specific than "Why Major."

    Step 5: Avoid AI-Generated Feel

    GT's AI policy: brainstorming/editing OK, but "your ultimate submission should be your own."

    How to ensure human voice:

    • Use first-person narrative moments ("When I...", "After I...")
    • Include specific numbers/details only Runchen knows ("8 hours to redesign", "30 seconds into qualification")
    • Let one sentence be slightly imperfect if it sounds authentic
    • Don't over-polish into generic smoothness
