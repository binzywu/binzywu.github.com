Predict → manipulate → explain

Test a biological model.

These simplified models use stated assumptions and synthetic data. They support reasoning; they do not replace hands-on lab investigations or establish real experimental results.

01 · Predict water movement

Compare water potentials across a water-permeable membrane. Solute is nonpenetrating, i=1, temperature is298 K and external pressure potential is0 bar. Internal pressure can model an opposing pressure in a walled cell. This shows an initial tendency, not a time simulation.

02 · Explore saturation and competitive inhibition

This illustrative rate model holds Vmax=100 rate units and uses v=Vmax·S/(αKm+S), where α≥1 represents competitive inhibition. The model is a quantitative extension, not an equation you must memorize for this toolkit’s AP-topic checks. It does not simulate pH, temperature or all inhibitor mechanisms.

Green: selected α · dashed orange: no inhibitor, α=1 · gray: Vmax. Substrate and Km use matching arbitrary concentration units.

03 · Build a one-locus inheritance model

Choose two diploid parent genotypes. The grid combines gamete draws, assuming equal segregation, random fertilization and equal viability. A is completely dominant to a for the phenotype summary. These predictions are probabilities, not guaranteed counts in a small family.

04 · Compare a null model with selection

Set allele frequency p, then change relative survival/reproductive contributions for AA,Aa and aa. The model starts with Hardy–Weinberg genotype frequencies, applies one generation of viability selection and computes p among survivors. It omits drift, migration and new mutation. Survivor genotypes need not remain in Hardy–Weinberg proportions.

Green: before selection · orange: normalized surviving genotypes. A label is an allele name, not a statement of fitness or dominance.

05 · Compare exponential and logistic growth

These continuous deterministic models start from N₀=20 with a constant per-capita parameter r. The logistic model also uses a fixed carrying capacity K. Real populations can have delays, stochasticity and changing resources; these curves are model predictions.

Green: logistic · dashed orange: exponential · gray: carrying capacity. A shared vertical scale can compress the logistic curve when exponential values are large.

06 · Test categorical counts with chi-square

A synthetic sample has100 independent observations in two mutually exclusive categories. Select a fixed null probability for category A, then vary its observed count; category B is the remainder. All expected counts here exceed5. No probabilities are estimated from the sample, so df=1.

Green: observed counts · orange: expected counts under the null model.

A useful study session

  1. Recall yesterday’s mechanism without notes.
  2. Read the idea, then predict each worked step.
  3. Draw or explain the paper problem before viewing the reasoning.
  4. Complete both checks. A lesson shows ✓ Completed after both are solved.
  5. Test one prediction in a related model or write a short evidence-based explanation.

Revisit mistakes later. Completing checks measures recorded practice, not mastery.

Six science-practice habits

  1. Explain: connect structures, processes and causes.
  2. Read models: interpret arrows, scales and limits.
  3. Design: name variables, controls and independent replicates.
  4. Represent data: choose axes, units and an appropriate display.
  5. Analyze: calculate, describe uncertainty and test a model.
  6. Argue from evidence: support a claim and acknowledge alternatives.
